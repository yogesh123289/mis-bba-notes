Knowing the name of an information system is not enough.

We need to understand:

- Who uses the system?
- Where does the system operate?
- What business activities does it support?
- What technology does it use?

# 🔥 Let's Begin With a Simple Question

Imagine your university has an ERP system.

Students use it to:

- Register for courses
- Check attendance
- View marks
- Submit examination forms
- Check fees
- Download documents

Faculty members use it to:

- Mark attendance
- Enter marks
- View student information
- Generate reports

Management may use it to:

- Monitor student performance
- Monitor attendance
- Analyze academic results
- Prepare institutional reports

Now think carefully:

### 🤔 Is the ERP system only a computer?

**No.**

### 🤔 Is it only software?

**No.**

### 🤔 Is it only data?

**No.**

### 🤔 Can the ERP work without students, faculty and administrators?

**No.**

### 🤔 Can it work without computers, networks and software?

**No.**

This gives us the first important idea:

> **An information system is much more than technology.**

It involves **people, organization, processes, data, and technology working together**.

# 1. What Are the Dimensions of MIS?

The **dimensions of MIS** represent the major perspectives that must be considered when understanding an information system.

The three commonly discussed dimensions are:

1. **Organization**
2. **People**
3. **Technology**

A simple way to remember them is:

```text
                INFORMATION SYSTEM
                         │
          ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
          │              │              │
          ▼              ▼              ▼
     ORGANIZATION      PEOPLE      TECHNOLOGY
```

These three dimensions work together.

An information system cannot be properly understood by looking at technology alone.

---

# 2. Why Are Dimensions Important?

Suppose a university buys the most advanced ERP software available.

The software is excellent.

The computers are powerful.

The network is fast.

But:

- Faculty members are not trained.
- Students do not understand how to use the system.
- University procedures are unclear.
- Departments do not coordinate.
- Management does not use the information properly.

### 🤔 Will the ERP be successful?

**Not necessarily.**

The technology may be excellent, but the overall information system may still fail.

Why?

Because an information system depends on more than technology.

It depends on:

> **Organization + People + Technology**

This is one of the most important ideas in MIS.

---

# 3. The Three Dimensions of MIS

The three dimensions can be understood as follows:

| Dimension | Simple Meaning | Main Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Organization** | How the business is structured and works | How does the organization operate? |
| **People** | Individuals who use, manage, and support the system | Who uses the system? |
| **Technology** | Tools used to collect, process, store, and communicate information | What technology is being used? |

### 🧠 Easy Memory Trick

Remember:

> **O – P – T**

**O = Organization**

**P = People**

**T = Technology**

# 4. Dimension 1 – Organization

The first dimension of MIS is **Organization**.

An information system exists within an organization.

Therefore, to understand an information system, we must first understand how the organization works.

---

# 5. What Does Organization Mean in MIS?

In MIS, organization refers to the way a business or institution:

- Structures its activities
- Divides responsibilities
- Performs business processes
- Makes decisions
- Communicates
- Follows rules
- Coordinates departments
- Serves customers or users

For example, a university may have:

```text
University
    │
    ├── Administration
    ├── Faculty / Departments
    ├── Finance
    ├── Examination
    ├── Admissions
    ├── Human Resources
    ├── Library
    └── IT / Technical Support
```

Each department performs different activities.

An information system helps these activities work together.

---

# 6. Organizations Have Different Levels

Organizations generally have different levels of responsibility.

A simple structure is:

```text
             TOP MANAGEMENT
                  │
          Strategic Decisions
                  │
                  ▼
          MIDDLE MANAGEMENT
                  │
          Tactical Decisions
                  │
                  ▼
       OPERATIONAL MANAGEMENT
                  │
         Daily Activities
```

Different levels require different information.

---

# 7. Top Management

Top management is responsible for the overall direction of the organization.

Examples include:

- CEO
- Managing Director
- University leadership
- Director
- Senior executives

They may ask questions such as:

> Are we growing?

> Are we achieving our goals?

> Should we enter a new market?

> Should we open another branch?

> Should we introduce a new service?

Therefore, they generally need **high-level and summarized information**.

---

# 8. Middle Management

Middle managers are responsible for departments, divisions, or specific areas.

Examples:

- Department Heads
- Branch Managers
- Sales Managers
- HR Managers
- Operations Managers

They may ask:

> Is my department performing well?

> Why are sales decreasing?

> Which employees require additional training?

> Which branch is performing better?

They require information that helps them **monitor and manage performance**.

---

# 9. Operational Management

Operational managers and employees are involved in everyday activities.

Examples:

- Cashiers
- Sales employees
- Administrative staff
- Customer service employees
- Data-entry staff

They deal with activities such as:

- Recording sales
- Updating records
- Processing payments
- Registering students
- Recording attendance
- Processing customer orders

Their information needs are usually more detailed and closely connected with daily activities.

---

# 11. Business Processes

Another important part of the organizational dimension is the **business process**.

A business process is simply:

> **A series of activities performed to achieve a particular business objective.**

Do not worry about the term "business process."

Think of it as:

> **How work actually gets done.**

---

# 12. Example of a Simple Business Process

Consider purchasing a product from an online store.

The process may look like:

```text
Customer Selects Product
          ↓
Adds Product to Cart
          ↓
Places Order
          ↓
Payment
          ↓
Order Confirmation
          ↓
Product Packed
          ↓
Product Shipped
          ↓
Product Delivered
```

This is a business process.

An information system can support many of these activities.

---

# 13. Why Are Business Processes Important in MIS?

Suppose an organization introduces new software but does not understand its existing business process.

Problems may occur.

For example:

- Information may be entered twice.
- Employees may not know who is responsible.
- Approval may take too long.
- Important information may be missed.
- Different departments may maintain separate records.

Therefore:

> **A good information system should support and improve business processes.**

---

- Eligibility can be checked.

- Student records can be created.

# 15. Organizational Structure

Another important part of the organizational dimension is **organizational structure**.

Organizational structure means:

> **How responsibilities, authority, and activities are arranged within an organization.**

For example:

```text
                UNIVERSITY
                    │
          ┌─────────┼─────────┐
          │         │         │
      Academic    Finance   Admin
          │
     Departments
          │
      Faculty
          │
      Students
```

The information system should support this structure.

---

# 16. Why Does Organizational Structure Matter?

Imagine a university where:

- Finance cannot access necessary fee information.
- Examination cannot access student registration information.
- Faculty cannot access the attendance system.
- Management cannot access required reports.

Even if every department has computers, the organization may still face serious problems.

Therefore:

> **Information must move between the right people and departments at the right time.**

---

# 17. Organizational Culture

Another important part of the organizational dimension is **organizational culture**.

Organizational culture means:

> **The shared ways of thinking, working, behaving, and communicating within an organization.**

For example, one organization may encourage:

- Innovation
- Experimentation
- Teamwork
- Open communication

Another organization may be more:

- Formal
- Rule-oriented
- Hierarchical
- Conservative

These differences can influence how employees accept and use information systems.

---

# 18. Example – New Attendance System

Suppose a university introduces a new digital attendance system.

### University A

Faculty members receive:

- Training
- Technical support
- Clear instructions
- Regular assistance

Faculty gradually accept the system.

### University B

The system is introduced without:

- Training
- Communication
- Support

Faculty members may resist using it.

### 🎯 Important Lesson

The problem may not be the technology.

The problem may be:

> **How the organization introduced and managed the change.**

---

# 19. Organizational Policies and Rules

Organizations also have policies and rules.

For example:

- Attendance policy
- Leave policy
- Examination policy
- Financial approval policy
- Data access policy
- Employee performance policy

Information systems must often support these rules.

For example:

If a university requires **75% attendance** for examination eligibility, the attendance system should be able to:

- Record attendance
- Calculate attendance percentage
- Identify students below the required level
- Generate reports

Therefore:

> **Organizational rules often influence how information systems are designed and used.**

# 22. 🎯 Think Like a Manager

Suppose a company buys a new CRM system.

The software is excellent.

But after six months, employees still do not use it properly.

Before blaming the software, a manager should ask:

1. Were employees trained?
2. Were business processes clearly defined?
3. Was the system suitable for the organization?
4. Did employees understand why the system was introduced?
5. Was management supporting the change?
6. Were organizational policies aligned with the system?

This is why MIS is not simply about buying technology.

# 26. Transition to the Next Dimension

We have now understood that an information system operates inside an organization.

But there is another important question:

> **Who actually uses the system?**

A system cannot achieve anything by itself.

Someone has to:

- Enter information
- Use information
- Manage the system
- Make decisions
- Solve problems
- Maintain the system
- Improve processes

This brings us to the second dimension of MIS:

# 👥 PEOPLE – The Second Dimension of MIS

People are one of the most important dimensions of an information system.

A technically excellent system is of little value if people do not know how to use it, do not trust it, or do not understand why it is needed.

Therefore:

> **Technology provides the tools, but people make the information system useful.**

# 29. Users of Information Systems

The people who interact with an information system can broadly be called **users**.

A user is simply:

> **A person who uses an information system to perform a task or obtain information.**

For example:

- A cashier using a billing system
- A student using a university ERP
- A customer using mobile banking
- A manager viewing a sales dashboard
- A faculty member entering attendance

All of them are users.

---

# 30. Operational Users

Operational users are people who work directly with day-to-day business activities.

Examples include:

- Cashiers
- Sales employees
- Receptionists
- Administrative staff
- Data-entry operators
- Customer service employees

They may use systems to:

- Enter data
- Update records
- Process transactions
- Check information
- Complete routine activities

### Example

A cashier scans products and generates a bill.

The cashier is an operational user of the organization's information system.

---

# 31. Managers as Users

Managers use information systems differently from operational employees.

A manager may not enter every individual transaction.

Instead, the manager may use information to:

- Monitor performance
- Identify problems
- Compare results
- Allocate resources
- Plan activities
- Make decisions

For example:

A sales manager may look at:

```text
Monthly Sales
        ↓
Branch Performance
        ↓
Product Performance
        ↓
Sales Trends
        ↓
Managerial Action
```

The manager uses information rather than simply recording transactions.

---

# 32. Senior Managers and Executives

Senior executives generally require information that helps them understand the organization's overall position.

They may examine:

- Revenue
- Profit
- Growth
- Customer trends
- Market performance
- Major risks
- Long-term trends

For example:

> "Is our company growing faster than our competitors?"

This requires a different type of information from the information required by a cashier.

---

# 33. IT Professionals

Information systems also require people who design, develop, maintain, and support them.

Examples include:

### System Analysts

They study organizational requirements and help determine what the system should do.

### Software Developers

They create or modify software applications.

### Database Administrators

They manage databases and help maintain data availability and security.

### Network Administrators

They manage and maintain communication networks.

### IT Support Staff

They help users solve technical problems.

# 34. System Analyst – Simple Explanation

A system analyst acts as a bridge between:

```text
BUSINESS PEOPLE
       ↕
SYSTEM / IT TEAM
```

Suppose a university tells the IT team:

> "We need a better student attendance system."

The system analyst may ask:

- What information needs to be recorded?
- Who will enter attendance?
- Who can view attendance?
- What reports are required?
- What rules should the system follow?
- What problems exist in the current process?

The analyst then helps translate these business requirements into system requirements.

---

# 35. Database Administrator – Simple Explanation

A database administrator works with the organization's databases.

Think of a database as a large, organized digital storage area.

For example, a university database may contain:

- Student information
- Faculty information
- Attendance
- Marks
- Fees
- Course information

The database administrator helps ensure that this information is:

- Available
- Organized
- Protected
- Properly maintained

# 38. Skills and Knowledge

People need appropriate skills to use information systems effectively.

For example, an employee using a complex reporting system may need training in:

- Basic computer use
- Data entry
- Report generation
- Data interpretation
- System procedures

Managers may need additional skills such as:

- Understanding reports
- Interpreting trends
- Comparing alternatives
- Using information for decisions

Therefore:

> **Having access to an information system does not automatically mean that people can use it effectively.**

---

# 39. Training and Development

When a new information system is introduced, employees often need training.

For example, suppose a company replaces its old payroll system.

Employees may need to learn:

- How to log in
- How to enter information
- How to correct errors
- How to generate reports
- How to follow new procedures

Without proper training, employees may:

- Make mistakes
- Avoid the system
- Depend heavily on others
- Continue using old methods

Therefore:

> **Training is an important part of successful information-system implementation.**

---

# 40. Human Resistance to Change

One of the biggest challenges when introducing a new information system is **resistance to change**.

People may think:

> "The old system was easier."

> "Why do we need a new system?"

> "I don't know how to use this."

> "This will increase my workload."

> "What if I make a mistake?"

This is normal human behavior.

---

- Demonstrations
- Technical support
- Clear instructions
- Time to adapt
- Feedback opportunities

- Fear of change
- Lack of communication
- Poor system design

Result:

> Users may lose access to shared systems.

### Procedure Failure

Employees do not follow proper steps.

Result:

> Errors and inconsistent work may occur.

### People Problem

Users are not trained or refuse to use the system.

Result:

> The system may not deliver expected benefits.
